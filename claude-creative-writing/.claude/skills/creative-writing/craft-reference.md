# Craft Reference

## Plot and Causality

- Connect beats with “therefore” or “but,” not merely “and then.”
- Escalate by narrowing choices, increasing cost, exposing vulnerability, or changing what the character believes.
- Pay off major promises in proportion to the attention given to them.

## Scene Design

A functional scene normally contains:

1. a viewpoint character with an immediate desire;
2. resistance that prevents an easy outcome;
3. tactics and choices revealing character;
4. a turn that changes the emotional or practical situation;
5. an ending that creates consequence, decision, or forward pressure.

Not every scene needs overt conflict. Discovery, intimacy, wonder, dread, and grief can create movement when the character's understanding changes.

## Characterization

- Define characters through choices made under pressure.
- Give major characters competing wants, contradictions, and personal logic.
- Separate voice through vocabulary, rhythm, attention, values, and avoidance.
- Preserve the difference between what a character wants, needs, says, and actually does.

## Point of View

- Filter observation through what the viewpoint character would notice and how they would interpret it.
- Maintain consistent psychic distance unless a shift is deliberate.
- Do not reveal information unavailable to the viewpoint character.
- Make viewpoint changes clear and structurally motivated.

## Dialogue

- Give each speaker an objective within the exchange.
- Use subtext when direct speech would be emotionally or strategically costly.
- Keep exposition responsive to conflict or curiosity.
- Use action beats when they reveal behavior or regulate pacing, not as random movement between lines.

## Description and Worldbuilding

- Select details that establish mood, function, social meaning, or character.
- Introduce world rules when they affect a choice or consequence.
- Prefer specific, usable detail to encyclopedic explanation.
- Track costs and limitations so magic and technology remain dramatically credible.

## Pacing

- Slow down for irreversible choices, emotional turns, discovery, and payoff.
- Compress travel, repetition, setup already understood, and low-consequence transitions.
- Use paragraphing, syntax, scene length, and information release to control reading speed.

## Worked Examples

Each pair shows a common failure and a revision. Read the diagnosis, not just the prose: the point is to recognize the habit in your own draft.

### Emotion: named versus dramatized

Weak:

> Mara felt a wave of overwhelming grief wash over her as she realized her brother was truly gone. The loss was devastating, and she could not believe he had left her alone in the world.

Revised:

> Mara carried his mug to the sink, rinsed it, and set it on the rack beside her own. Then she took it down again and put it back in the cupboard, at the back, where the chipped ones lived.

Diagnosis: the first version names the feeling three times and leaves the reader nothing to do. The second names no feeling at all. Grief arrives through a hand changing its mind mid-motion, and the reader supplies the word.

### Dialogue: information exchange versus competing objectives

Weak:

> "As you know, the shipment arrives Thursday," Devlin said.
> "Yes, and the harbor inspector has been asking questions about our manifests," Cole replied.
> "That is concerning. We should be careful," Devlin said.

Revised:

> "Thursday," Devlin said. "You'll want to be somewhere else."
>
> Cole turned the manifest over, as though the back of it might say something different. "Ellery's been down at the harbor three days running."
>
> "Asking about what?"
>
> "He didn't ask me." Cole set the page down. "That's what I keep thinking about."

Diagnosis: in the first version both men already know everything they say, so the dialogue exists only to inform the reader. In the second, Devlin wants Cole clear of Thursday, Cole wants to raise the danger without accusing anyone, and neither says so. The information still lands — later, and through pressure.

### Viewpoint: filtered versus direct

Weak:

> She saw the door at the end of the corridor and noticed that it was open. She realized someone had been inside.

Revised:

> The door at the end of the corridor stood open. She had locked it.

Diagnosis: `saw`, `noticed`, and `realized` narrate the act of perceiving instead of the thing perceived, pushing the reader one step back from the viewpoint. Cutting the filters closes the distance, and the second sentence lets the conclusion stay unstated.

### Prose: default register versus specific detail

Weak:

> In a world where everything had changed, the city stood silent. It was not just a place — it was a memory, a promise, a wound. Something shifted in the air, and she knew, deep down, that nothing would ever be the same again.

Revised:

> The trams had stopped running in March, and by June the rails had gone bright with rust in some places and green in others. She walked down the middle of Kossuth Street because there was no longer any reason not to.

Diagnosis: the first version is assembled from defaults — the "in a world where" opening, the "not just X — it was A, B, C" trio, the portentous air, the unearned finality. None of it is specific to this city or this woman. The revision replaces all of it with two verifiable facts and one changed behavior, and the desolation follows from them.
